Why does my puppy bite so much?

Because they are a puppy, and because their mouth is how they meet the world. That is the honest answer, and it does not make the evenings easier.

Biting usually starts to ease somewhere between four and six months, when the adult teeth come through and the puppy learns that teeth end the game. Until then it is normal, and the job is to teach a soft mouth rather than to stop mouthing altogether.

What is normal, and when

Puppies explore with their mouths from the week they can walk. The intensity climbs between eight and sixteen weeks, which is also when the baby teeth start to loosen, and then falls away over the next couple of months.

AgeWhat is happening
3 to 4 weeksBaby teeth come through. Play with littermates teaches the first bite limits
8 to 12 weeksMouthing peaks. Everything is tested with teeth
12 to 16 weeksBaby teeth start to fall out. Chewing increases
4 to 6 monthsAdult teeth arrive. Most mouthing fades
6 to 7 monthsFull adult set. Biting at this age is usually habit or over-arousal, not teething

The five reasons it happens

  • Exploring. Mouthing is how a puppy checks what a thing is, the way we use hands.
  • Teething. Sore gums want pressure. This is when a frozen carrot earns its keep.
  • Being overtired. The hardest biting of the day is usually in the evening, from a puppy who has been awake too long.
  • Being overexcited. Fast play, chasing and squealing all raise the arousal, and teeth come out at the top of it.
  • Needing something. A puppy who is hungry, thirsty or needs to toilet often gets mouthy first.

If you can name which one you are looking at, you already know what to do: sleep, a chew, a toilet trip, or the end of the game.

What works

  • End the game. The moment teeth touch skin, stand up, fold your arms and look away for ten seconds. Then offer a toy. Puppies learn fast that teeth stop the fun.
  • Redirect to something legal. A chew, a stuffed toy, a frozen carrot. Have one within reach in every room for a month.
  • Give a short break. If they cannot settle, twenty seconds behind a baby gate or in a pen, then try again. Not a punishment, a reset.
  • Put them to bed. If it is the evening and nothing is working, the answer is usually sleep. This is the single most useful thing to know.

What backfires

  • Holding the muzzle shut. It teaches nothing and many puppies find it frightening.
  • Yelping loudly. It works for some puppies and winds others up further. If two attempts make it worse, stop.
  • Punishing after the fact. A puppy cannot connect a telling-off to something that happened a minute ago.
  • Rough hands. Pushing, tapping or grabbing at the face turns a game into a wrestling match, and the teeth come out harder.

Teaching a soft mouth

The goal is not a puppy who never uses teeth. It is a puppy who controls the pressure. That lesson comes from play that stops when it hurts and restarts when it is gentle, repeated a hundred times over a few weeks.

It also comes from other puppies. A well-run puppy class with supervised play teaches bite inhibition faster than any human can, because the feedback is instant and comes from someone the same size.

When it is worth asking for help

Talk to a force-free trainer or your vet if a puppy is drawing blood repeatedly past six months, if the biting comes with a hard stare and a stiff body rather than a wagging one, if it happens around food or a favourite toy, or if a previously gentle adult dog starts biting. Those are different problems from puppy mouthing, and they deserve eyes on the situation rather than another article.
